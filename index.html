Consensus, Incentives, and the Limits of Soft-Fork Governance

An Analysis of the BIP-110 Conflict and Its Implications for Bitcoin

Abstract

In August 2026, BIP-110 (Reduced Data Temporary Softfork) entered its mandatory signaling window with approximately 2.5 percent miner support. Nodes enforcing the proposal rejected non-signaling blocks and formed a minority chain that produced only two blocks before stalling, while the dominant chain continued under existing consensus rules. This paper analyzes the technical design of BIP-110, the incentive asymmetry it sought to address, the game-theoretic consequences of its activation parameters, and the historical context that shaped both the proposal and its rejection. It argues that activation under such low legitimacy would have damaged Bitcoin’s consensus process more severely than the observed outcome, yet the episode left unresolved a genuine principal-agent problem between miners, who capture one-time fees, and node operators, who bear permanent storage and legal costs. After examining precedents from the block-size wars and earlier data debates, the paper evaluates the result against Bitcoin’s coordination mechanisms and design priorities. It concludes that the main-chain rejection correctly preserved high legitimacy thresholds and neutrality, while the underlying externality remains a structural vulnerability. A stricter framework for any future consensus-level response to resource externalities is outlined.

Introduction

Bitcoin’s consensus rules are intentionally difficult to alter. This difficulty is not a defect, it is a central security and governance feature. A soft fork that changes the definition of validity binds every full node and every economic actor who treats the chain as Bitcoin. When such a change is proposed with narrow support, the network must choose between retaining the existing rule set and accepting the risk of fragmentation or eroded legitimacy.

BIP-110 forced exactly this choice. Formally named the Reduced Data Temporary Softfork and published under the pseudonym Dathon Ohm, the proposal aimed to impose a temporary set of consensus restrictions on several techniques used to embed arbitrary data in Bitcoin transactions. Its supporters argued that inscriptions, large OP_RETURN payloads, and related constructions had converted a material portion of blockspace into permanent non-monetary storage. They emphasized a structural asymmetry: miners receive a one-time fee for inclusion, while full nodes permanently store the data and absorb associated costs, including potential legal exposure to illegal content. The proposal was framed as a limited, expiring intention intended to protect Bitcoin’s function as money and the long-term viability of independent node operation.

Critics rejected both the diagnosis and the remedy. They contended that BIP-110 encoded contested value judgments about legitimate uses of blockspace into consensus rules, that its activation design was reckless, and that permissionless neutrality is more foundational to Bitcoin than any particular vision of monetary purity. On 9 August 2026 the mandatory window began at block 961,632. Enforcing nodes diverged onto a minority tip that advanced only two blocks. The dominant chain, backed by the vast majority of hashrate, continued without interruption. The economically relevant network therefore retained its prior rules.

This paper treats the BIP-110 conflict as a case study in Bitcoin’s contemporary governance. It does not presuppose that either arbitrary data is inherently harmful or any data limit constitutes censorship is self-evidently true. Instead it examines the proposal’s technical mechanics, the incentive misalignment it attempted to correct, the strategic consequences of its activation parameters, and the historical patterns that informed both sides. The central claims are two. First, activation of BIP-110 under the observed levels of support would have inflicted greater damage on Bitcoin’s consensus legitimacy than the rejection that occurred. Second, the rejection left intact a real externality, permanent data costs and legal risks borne by node operators, that will continue to generate pressure. The final sections therefore derive stricter principles that any future attempt to address resource externalities at the consensus layer should satisfy.

The analysis moves from technical description through incentive and game-theoretic examination, historical comparison, normative assessment, and concrete recommendations. The focus throughout remains the durability of Bitcoin’s coordination mechanisms rather than the short-term success or failure of any single faction.

Technical Overview of BIP-110

BIP-110, formally titled the Reduced Data Temporary Softfork, proposed a time-limited set of additional consensus rules intended to restrict several methods of embedding arbitrary data in Bitcoin transactions. The proposal was authored under the pseudonym Dathon Ohm and first circulated in late 2025 before receiving its BIP number. It was designed as a soft fork: the new rules would be stricter than existing consensus rules, rendering certain previously valid transactions invalid, while remaining backward compatible for nodes that did not enforce them.

The core restrictions targeted the primary techniques used by Ordinals inscriptions, large OP_RETURN payloads, BRC-20 style tokens, and related constructions. Under the proposed rules, most new transaction outputs would be limited to 34 bytes, with an exception allowing OP_RETURN outputs up to 83 bytes. Data pushes and certain witness stack elements would be capped at 256 bytes. Additional constraints applied to Taproot: annexes would be disallowed, control blocks would face size limits that effectively restricted deep script trees, and specific opcodes including OP_IF and OP_NOTIF within Tapscripts would be restricted. Undefined witness versions would become unspendable during the active period.

A critical design choice was the treatment of existing outputs. Any UTXO created before activation would remain permanently exempt from the new rules. This grandfathering provision ensured that pre-existing funds could still be spent under the prior validity conditions and removed the risk of freezing coins. The restrictions themselves were temporary. Once activated, the additional rules would remain in force for 52,416 blocks, approximately one year, after which they would expire automatically and the prior consensus rules would resume.

The proposal did not attempt to prohibit all forms of data embedding. Its authors acknowledged that determined users could still place small amounts of data within the remaining allowed fields. The stated objective was to raise the cost and reduce the practicality of large-scale or inscription-style data storage while leaving ordinary monetary transactions, including standard Taproot key-path spends, unaffected. In technical terms, BIP-110 sought to reclassify certain high-data constructions from consensus-valid to consensus-invalid for a bounded period, thereby shifting the burden of permanent storage away from the full node network during that window.

Activation Mechanism and Design Choices

BIP-110 employed a modified version of the BIP9 version-bits signaling framework. Miners were expected to indicate support by setting version bit 4 in the blocks they produced. Under standard BIP9 practice, a soft fork typically requires a high threshold, commonly 95 percent of blocks within a difficulty adjustment period of 2,016 blocks, before lock-in can occur. BIP-110 deliberately lowered this threshold to 55 percent, equivalent to 1,109 signaling blocks out of 2,016. The designers treated the reduced threshold as necessary because miner incentives were expected to conflict with the proposal’s goal of limiting high-data transactions that generate fees.

If the 55 percent threshold was never reached during ordinary signaling, the proposal included a mandatory signaling window. This window was scheduled to begin at block height 961,632, which arrived in early August 2026. During the mandatory window, any node enforcing BIP-110 was required to treat blocks that did not set version bit 4 as invalid. The window was defined to run through block 963,647. Lock-in was scheduled to occur no later than block 963,648 if the mandatory phase completed, with the new consensus rules becoming active at block 965,664. Once active, the additional restrictions would remain in force for 52,416 blocks before automatic expiry.

These parameters produced a distinctive activation profile. In the difficulty period immediately preceding the mandatory window, only 51 blocks signaled support, corresponding to roughly 2.53 percent of the period. When block 961,632 arrived, enforcing nodes began rejecting non-signaling blocks and followed an alternative tip produced by a small number of miners associated with the Ocean pool. That minority tip advanced only two blocks. The dominant chain, which continued to accept non-signaling blocks under existing rules, retained virtually all hashrate and economic activity.

The design choices reflected a specific theory of miner behavior. By setting a lower threshold and adding a mandatory rejection phase, the authors attempted to give node operators a mechanism that could not be indefinitely deferred by fee-maximizing hashrate. In practice the mechanism converted a low-support proposal into a temporary chain split. Nodes that enforcing the new validity rules isolated themselves from the heaviest chain, while the economic majority remained on the prior rule set. The activation design therefore succeeded in creating a visible divergence but failed to move the economically relevant network onto the proposed rules. This outcome followed directly from the interaction between the lowered threshold, the mandatory window, and the actual distribution of hashrate.

The Incentive Misalignment Between Miners and Node Operators

The central economic claim advanced by supporters of BIP-110 rests on a structural asymmetry between the parties who include data and the parties who permanently store it. Miners receive a one-time fee for incorporating a transaction into a block. Once that block is accepted by the network, the data it contains becomes part of the permanent ledger. Full nodes, particularly those that retain the complete historical chain, thereafter bear the ongoing costs of storage, bandwidth, verification, and potential legal exposure associated with that data. The private benefit of inclusion is captured at the moment of mining; the long-term costs are distributed across the set of validating and archival nodes.

This arrangement constitutes a classic externality. The fee market prices the scarce resource of blockspace at the time of inclusion and thereby rations access according to willingness to pay. It does not, however, incorporate the permanent marginal cost imposed on every node that continues to store and serve the data. A transaction that embeds a large arbitrary payload can therefore be rational for both the data publisher and the miner while remaining collectively costly for the node network. Over repeated instances the cumulative effect raises the resource requirements of running a fully validating node and increases the surface area for legal or regulatory pressure, especially in jurisdictions that treat certain categories of stored content as prohibited.

Local policy tools available to node operators mitigate but do not eliminate the problem. Mempool and relay filters can prevent a node from propagating or accepting certain transactions into its own view of the mempool. They cannot prevent a miner from including a consensus-valid, high-fee transaction directly into a block. Once such a block is produced and accumulates sufficient proof-of-work, nodes that wish to remain on the heaviest valid chain must accept the data. Pruning reduces storage for some nodes yet does not remove the data from the network as a whole, nor does it eliminate legal questions that may attach to historical chain data. Alternative clients with stricter default policies shift individual node behavior without binding the miners who collect fees under the prevailing consensus rules.

BIP-110 treated this asymmetry as sufficiently severe to justify a temporary consensus intervention. By rendering certain high-data constructions invalid for a bounded period, the proposal sought to prevent miners from collecting fees on those constructions and thereby to reduce the volume of permanent data imposed on the node set. Critics of the proposal countered that the externality, while real, does not automatically warrant altering validity rules for the entire network, and that the availability of policy, pruning, and client choice already supplies partial remedies. The disagreement therefore turns less on the existence of divergent incentives than on the appropriate institutional layer at which those incentives should be addressed. The August 2026 outcome left the underlying asymmetry intact: miners retained the ability to include high-data transactions for fees, and node operators retained the permanent costs associated with that inclusion.

Data Storage Externalities and Legal-Risk Arguments

Beyond the pure resource costs of storage and bandwidth, BIP-110 supporters advanced a second and more politically charged externality: the legal risk that accompanies permanent storage of arbitrary data. Because Bitcoin’s ledger is append-only and globally replicated among archival nodes, any content successfully embedded in a mined block becomes part of the historical record that those nodes retain. When that content includes material prohibited under the laws of a given jurisdiction, particularly child sexual abuse material, every node that stores the full chain may be exposed to possession or distribution liability under local statutes.

This risk is asymmetric in two respects. First, the data publisher and the miner who collected the fee face no ongoing storage obligation after inclusion, while archival nodes do. Second, legal exposure is jurisdiction-dependent. A node operator in a jurisdiction with aggressive enforcement faces a different risk profile from one in a more permissive environment, yet both store the same global dataset. Supporters of BIP-110 argued that this combination creates a slow centralizing pressure: as the volume of potentially illicit data grows, the set of individuals and institutions willing to run fully validating archival nodes shrinks, leaving validation increasingly concentrated among larger, professionally managed, or geographically selective operators.

The proposal treated temporary consensus limits as a partial mitigation. By raising the cost of large-scale data embedding for a defined period, the rules would reduce the rate at which new high-risk material entered the permanent ledger. Grandfathering of pre-activation outputs ensured that existing data remained spendable, while the automatic expiry after approximately one year limited the duration of the intervention. In this framing, the legal risk was not a hypothetical edge case but a structural threat to the breadth of the node network itself.

Critics of the legal-risk argument advanced several responses. Bitcoin’s consensus rules cannot be optimized for the criminal codes of every jurisdiction simultaneously; attempting to do so imports external political and moral criteria into the definition of validity. Nodes already possess technical means to limit their exposure, including pruning historical data, running non-archival configurations, or operating in jurisdictions with different legal standards. The presence of illegal content on the chain predated BIP-110 and was not created by the failure of the proposal. Finally, using consensus rules to address legal liability risks establishing a precedent whereby future pressure groups demand protocol changes to remove or restrict other categories of disfavored data. In this view, the appropriate responses remain policy filters, client choice, and legal defenses available to node operators rather than a network-wide alteration of validity conditions.

The August 2026 outcome left both the resource externality and the legal-risk externality unaddressed at the consensus layer. Whether the magnitude of these risks justifies future intervention, and at what institutional level, remains an open question that subsequent sections examine through historical and game-theoretic lenses.

Historical Precedents and Prior Spam Waves

Bitcoin has confronted resource and data-abuse questions since its earliest years. Satoshi Nakamoto and the initial developers introduced practical limits on dust outputs and related anti-spam measures when low-value transaction floods threatened to degrade network performance. These early interventions operated primarily through policy and node software defaults rather than through repeated consensus changes. They established a pattern in which clear resource attacks were met with pragmatic restrictions while the definition of validity itself remained stable.

The history of OP_RETURN illustrates the same tension in more recent form. OP_RETURN was introduced as a provably unspendable output type intended for small data carriers. For years its size was constrained by standardness policy rather than by consensus rules. Periodic proposals to raise or remove those policy limits generated debate about whether Bitcoin should facilitate greater data embedding. In 2025, Bitcoin Core’s default relay policy was relaxed in a manner that BIP-110 supporters later cited as removing a soft guardrail and contributing to increased non-monetary data volume. The subsequent push for consensus-level limits can therefore be read as a reaction to a perceived shift from policy restraint toward greater accommodation of arbitrary data.

The most consequential precedent remains the block-size wars of 2015 to 2017. That conflict centered on whether to increase the block weight limit to raise transaction throughput. Advocates of larger blocks argued that capacity constraints were artificially suppressing Bitcoin’s growth as a payment network. Opponents emphasized the resulting increase in node resource requirements and the risk of centralizing validation among well-resourced operators. The eventual outcome on the dominant chain was the activation of SegWit under conditions of broad support, accompanied by a hard-fork split that created Bitcoin Cash. The episode demonstrated two enduring lessons. First, attempts to force a resource-related rule change against substantial opposition produce lasting fragmentation and competing network effects. Second, the chain that preserved higher legitimacy thresholds for consensus changes retained the overwhelming share of economic activity, security budget, and brand recognition.

BIP-110 both drew upon and departed from these earlier episodes. Like the block-size debate, it concerned the allocation of a scarce resource and the distribution of long-term costs onto node operators. Unlike that debate, it proposed a temporary soft fork rather than a permanent capacity expansion, and it targeted specific data-carrying constructions rather than the overall block size. The rise of Ordinals and related protocols after 2022 supplied the immediate empirical backdrop: measurable increases in non-monetary data volume, periodic fee spikes, and renewed arguments over whether such usage constituted legitimate market demand or a form of spam that externalized costs. BIP-110’s authors presented their proposal as a limited corrective within this longer pattern of resource disputes. Its critics viewed it as a revival of the same impulse to encode contested preferences about proper usage into the consensus rules, an impulse that the block-size wars had already shown to be destabilizing when pursued without broad agreement.

Game-Theoretic Analysis of Signaling and Thresholds

The activation design of BIP-110 can be analyzed as a coordination game under incomplete alignment of payoffs. In the standard BIP9 framework, a high signaling threshold, typically 95 percent of blocks within a 2,016-block difficulty period, functions as a coordination device. It requires that a large fraction of hashrate visibly commit to the new rules before lock-in. The high bar reduces the probability of activation without rough consensus and thereby lowers the expected cost of a chain split. Miners who signal do so in the knowledge that activation is unlikely unless a substantial coalition forms. Nodes that prepare to enforce the new rules can anticipate that the economically relevant chain will move with them if the threshold is met.

BIP-110 altered this structure by lowering the threshold to 55 percent and by adding a mandatory signaling window. The reduced threshold changes the point at which a minority of hashrate can trigger lock-in under ordinary conditions. More importantly, the mandatory window converts the later phase of the process into a forced divergence for any node that elects to enforce the proposal. Once block 961,632 is reached, enforcing nodes must reject blocks that do not set the designated version bit. This rule creates a sharp strategic choice: either abandon the proposal or leave the heaviest chain.

Miner payoffs remain straightforward. Rational miners maximize the sum of block subsidy and transaction fees. A proposal that renders a class of currently high-fee transactions invalid reduces expected revenue for any miner who expects those transactions to continue. Lowering the signaling threshold does not alter this payoff; it only changes the mechanical conditions under which a low-support coalition can force enforcing nodes off the main chain. In the period preceding the mandatory window, signaling remained near 2.5 percent. This level is consistent with a dominant strategy of non-signaling for the large majority of hashrate.

Node operators who supported the proposal faced a different calculation. By running enforcing software they could create a visible alternative tip and demonstrate commitment. Without accompanying hashrate, however, that tip cannot accumulate work at a competitive rate. The heaviest-chain rule then ensures that economic activity and subsequent mining remain on the non-enforcing chain. The two-block minority chain observed in August 2026 is the equilibrium outcome of this interaction: a low-support coalition exercises the mandatory window, produces a short-lived alternative history, and is left behind by the accumulation of work on the dominant chain.

The Minority Chain Outcome and What It Revealed

The mandatory signaling window of BIP-110 opened at block 961,632 in early August 2026. Nodes configured to enforce the proposal began rejecting blocks that did not set version bit 4. A non-signaling block produced by a major pool was accepted by the dominant network and rejected by enforcing nodes, producing an immediate divergence. An alternative block mined through the Ocean pool’s infrastructure and associated with the Roughnecks group became the first tip of the minority chain. That chain advanced only one additional block before stalling. Within hours the dominant chain had extended by dozens of blocks; within a day the gap measured in the hundreds. No significant recovery of hashrate to the minority tip occurred.

The distribution of work made the result inevitable under Bitcoin’s existing consensus rules. In the difficulty period preceding the window, only 51 of 2,016 blocks had signaled support, or approximately 2.53 percent. Once the window opened, the overwhelming majority of hashrate continued to produce non-signaling blocks. The minority chain inherited the full difficulty target of the parent chain while commanding only a negligible fraction of the network’s computational power. Under the heaviest-chain selection rule, the dominant chain accumulated work at a vastly higher rate and rapidly outpaced the alternative history.

Economic recognition followed hashrate. No major exchange, custodian, or payment service publicly treated the minority tip as the continuation of Bitcoin. Liquidity, price discovery, and wallet infrastructure remained on the dominant chain. The absence of economic nodes willing to credit the minority history ensured that even the limited hashrate that initially appeared had little incentive to remain. Some supporters of the proposal subsequently discussed altering the proof-of-work algorithm on the stalled branch in order to create a viable alternative chain. That discussion itself illustrated how quickly a failed soft-fork attempt can escalate toward explicit chain separation.

The episode revealed the practical hierarchy of Bitcoin’s coordination mechanisms with unusual clarity. Hashrate determines the accumulation of work. Economic nodes determine which history carries value and liquidity. Enforcing nodes that adopt alternative validity rules without corresponding hashrate and economic follow-through can produce a visible fork, yet they cannot compel the network to adopt their rules. The two-block minority chain demonstrated that a low-support coalition can force a temporary partition when activation parameters include a mandatory rejection window, but that such a partition remains inert without broader support. Successful soft forks such as SegWit and Taproot had previously combined high signaling levels with continuous economic activity on the activating chain. BIP-110 produced the opposite configuration: low signaling, forced divergence, and rapid economic irrelevance of the alternative tip. The outcome therefore confirmed both the power of the heaviest-chain rule and the limited leverage available to node operators who lack hashrate and economic alignment.

Philosophical Divide: Monetary Purpose vs. Neutrality

Beneath the technical and game-theoretic disputes surrounding BIP-110 lay a deeper disagreement about the nature and priorities of Bitcoin itself. Two coherent but incompatible philosophical framings shaped the arguments on each side.

The first framing, advanced most clearly by supporters of the proposal, holds that Bitcoin’s primary purpose is to function as peer-to-peer electronic cash and sound money. On this view, the proposal’s value derives from its ability to transfer and store monetary value without trusted intermediaries, under conditions of credible scarcity and decentralized validation. Non-monetary uses of blockspace are tolerable only insofar as they do not undermine the conditions that sustain that monetary function. When large volumes of arbitrary data raise the long-term costs of running fully validating nodes, distort fee markets away from payments, or introduce legal risks that discourage independent node operation, those uses become a threat to the system’s core purpose. Temporary consensus limits are then justified as a defensive measure to preserve the monetary character of the network. Neutrality remains important, yet it is instrumental rather than absolute: neutrality that systematically erodes the node network ceases to serve the reasons Bitcoin was created.

The opposing framing treats permissionless neutrality as foundational rather than instrumental. Bitcoin’s defining property is the uniform enforcement of consensus rules without regard to the semantic content, social desirability, or economic category of valid transactions. Once the protocol begins distinguishing monetary from non-monetary data at the level of validity, it creates a permanent political interface through which future actors can demand further restrictions. Fee-paying use of blockspace, whatever its purpose, is legitimate under the existing rules. Node operators who object to particular uses retain the tools of policy, client choice, and pruning; they do not acquire the right to rewrite validity conditions for the entire network. On this view, the monetary properties of Bitcoin emerge from, and remain dependent upon, the refusal to let contested preferences about proper usage alter the base layer. Sacrificing neutrality in the name of monetary purity risks destroying the very feature that makes the money trustworthy.

These two framings interpret the same empirical observations through different lenses. Fee spikes associated with inscriptions appear to one side as evidence of harmful spam crowding out payments, and to the other as evidence of market demand for blockspace. Rising node costs appear to one side as an existential threat to decentralization, and to the other as a manageable resource pressure already addressed by policy and hardware improvements. Legal risk from illicit content appear to one side as a structural attack vector requiring consensus response, and to the other as a jurisdiction-specific problem that cannot be solved by altering global validity rules without importing external politics into the protocol.

The August 2026 outcome represented a practical victory for the neutrality framing: the economically relevant chain retained its existing rules and refused a low-support attempt to encode data preferences into consensus. The underlying philosophical disagreement, however, was not resolved. Both conceptions of Bitcoin continue to command adherents, and the tension between them remains available to structure future conflicts over resource use, node costs, and the proper scope of consensus rules.

Soft Power of Core, Alternative Clients, and Economic Nodes

Bitcoin’s governance cannot be reduced to hashrate alone. Software clients and the economic actors who run them exercise forms of soft power that shape which rules are practical to enforce and which histories carry value. The BIP-110 conflict illustrated both the reach and the limits of that soft power.

Bitcoin Core remains the dominant reference implementation. The large majority of listening nodes, mining pool software, exchange infrastructure, and wallet backends run Core or closely related code. Its maintainers therefore influence default relay policy, mempool behavior, and the practical definition of standard transactions. Decisions about whether to relax or tighten policy defaults, as occurred with OP_RETURN limits in the period preceding BIP-110, alter the cost and visibility of certain data-carrying constructions without changing consensus rules. Core’s refusal to implement or endorse BIP-110 meant that the proposal lacked the default-client pathway that has historically accompanied successful soft forks. This absence significantly constrained the proposal’s reach among operators who follow reference releases.

Alternative clients supplied the principal enforcement path for BIP-110. Bitcoin Knots, which maintains more restrictive default policies on data and relay, and related patched versions provided the software that enforcing nodes ran during the mandatory window. Client diversity therefore enabled a visible minority of operators to adopt alternative validity rules and to produce the short-lived minority chain. The episode demonstrated that alternative implementations can translate philosophical disagreement into concrete network divergence. At the same time, it showed the strict limit of that capacity: without accompanying hashrate and economic recognition, the alternative client’s rules remain confined to a low-work tip that the broader network ignores.

Hashrate and Economic Majority as Coordination Mechanisms

Bitcoin coordinates on a single history through the interaction of two mechanisms: the accumulation of proof-of-work under the heaviest-chain rule and the assignment of economic value by actors who accept that history. The BIP-110 episode provided a clear demonstration of both mechanisms operating under conditions of deliberate rule divergence.

The heaviest-chain rule selects, among competing valid histories, the one that embodies the greatest cumulative proof-of-work. Hashrate is the practical expression of this rule. Miners expend computational resources to extend a chosen tip; the tip that receives the preponderance of that expenditure pulls ahead in accumulated work and becomes increasingly costly to overtake. In ordinary operation the rule produces rapid convergence. When a subset of nodes adopts alternative validity conditions, the rule continues to operate without modification: the history that receives the preponderance of hashrate outpaces any alternative, regardless of the philosophical merits claimed for the alternative rules.

The economic majority supplies the complementary mechanism. Exchanges, custodians, payment processors, and holders determine which history is treated as Bitcoin for purposes of account balances, trade settlement, and price discovery. Their collective recognition converts a chain tip from a technical artifact into an economic asset. Without that recognition a tip may continue to accumulate occasional blocks yet remains irrelevant to the monetary system Bitcoin is intended to support. Economic nodes, in turn, have strong incentives to follow the history that already commands hashrate and liquidity, reinforcing the focal point created by proof-of-work.

Critiques of the 55% Threshold and Mandatory Signaling

The activation parameters chosen for BIP-110 departed from prior soft-fork practice in two material respects: the reduction of the signaling threshold from the conventional range of 90 to 95 percent to 55 percent, and the introduction of a mandatory signaling window that required enforcing nodes to reject non-signaling blocks. Both choices have been subject to sustained criticism on grounds of legitimacy, split risk, and long-term governance incentives.

High thresholds in the BIP9 framework serve a coordination function. By requiring that a large majority of hashrate visibly commit to new rules before lock-in, they make activation improbable without rough consensus. The high bar reduces the probability that a soft fork will proceed while a substantial fraction of the network remains opposed, and it thereby limits the expected cost of chain splits. Miners and economic nodes can treat signaling as informative precisely because activation is unlikely to occur on the basis of a narrow coalition.

BIP-110’s 55 percent threshold inverted this logic. The lower bar was selected because miner incentives were expected to conflict with the proposal’s aim of restricting high-data, fee-generating transactions. In effect the design treated miner resistance as a problem to be engineered around rather than as information about the absence of consensus. A threshold that can be met by a bare majority of hashrate, or that can be bypassed entirely by a mandatory window, transforms activation from a demonstration of broad agreement into a mechanical possibility under partial support.

Long-Term Governance and Ossification Risks

The BIP-110 episode carries consequences that extend beyond the immediate fate of one proposal. Bitcoin’s governance faces two opposing long-term risks: excessive ossification that prevents adaptation to genuine structural problems, and erosion of legitimacy standards that makes contentious rule changes easier to initiate and more damaging when they fail. The events of August 2026 interacted with both risks simultaneously.

On one side, the decisive rejection of a low-support soft fork reinforces the practical requirement of broad agreement before consensus rules can change. High legitimacy thresholds protect the network from restricted political contests over the definition of validity. They preserve the focal quality of the existing rule set and reduce the frequency of disruptive partitions. In this respect the outcome aligned with a conservative governance posture that treats stability of the rule set as a core security property. Yet the same posture, if applied without regard to the underlying problem the proposal sought to address, contributes to ossification. The permanent data-cost and legal-risk externalities that motivated BIP-110 remain in place. If every future attempt to confront those externalities at the consensus layer is met with the same combination of miner resistance and high procedural barriers, the node network may continue to absorb rising costs without institutional recourse.

Evaluation: Was the Outcome Net Positive for Bitcoin?

The central evaluative question is whether the events of August 2026 left Bitcoin better or worse positioned than the principal counterfactual: activation of BIP-110 under the observed levels of support. A balanced assessment must separate the effects of the rejection itself from the effects of the process that produced it and from the structural problem left unresolved.

The rejection itself carried several positive consequences. Consensus rules on the economically relevant chain remained unchanged despite a determined attempt to alter them with approximately 2.5 percent prior signaling. High legitimacy thresholds for soft-fork activation were thereby reinforced in practice. The primacy of hashrate and economic majority as coordination mechanisms was demonstrated with unusual clarity: a minority of enforcing nodes could force a visible divergence, yet they could not compel the network to adopt their rules or to assign economic value to the alternative history. Permissionless neutrality with respect to the semantic content of valid transactions was preserved on the dominant chain. Relative to a counterfactual in which contested data limits became binding on the main chain under narrow support, the observed outcome avoided a more serious erosion of consensus legitimacy.

Weighing these elements, the outcome was net positive relative to activation under low legitimacy. A soft fork that alters validity conditions for the entire network requires broad and durable support. BIP-110 never approached that condition. Allowing it to bind the dominant chain would have set a precedent more damaging to Bitcoin’s coordination mechanisms than the short-lived minority tip that actually occurred. At the same time, the episode was not costless. It raised the baseline expense of future disagreement and left a genuine structural tension intact. The net result is therefore best characterized as a defensive success for existing legitimacy standards, accompanied by unresolved pressures that will continue to test those standards in subsequent cycles of debate.

Persistent Structural Problems Left Unresolved

As detailed in the mechanic and economic syntheses of Sections 3 and 4, the core externality remains intact: miners capture one-time fee revenue while archival nodes absorb permanent storage and legal-risk burdens. Because local policy filters and alternative client defaults cannot prevent miners from including valid high-data transactions into blocks required by the heaviest-chain rule, the network continues to absorb unpriced resource externalities without a systemic coordination mechanism.

Design Intent and Satoshi-Era Principles

Any evaluation of contemporary consensus conflicts eventually turns to the design priorities visible in Bitcoin’s earliest documents and decisions. The whitepaper, Satoshi Nakamoto’s public correspondence, and the initial implementation choices supply the relevant evidence. These sources do not dictate a unique resolution to the BIP-110 dispute, yet they constrain the range of interpretations that can claim continuity with the system’s founding logic.

The whitepaper defines Bitcoin as a peer-to-peer electronic cash system whose principal technical problem is double-spending without a trusted intermediary. Proof-of-work and the heaviest-chain rule supply the coordination mechanism that allows distributed nodes to converge on a single history. Nodes independently verify every transaction and block against the prevailing rules; hashrate makes the selected history costly to revise. The architecture therefore privileges two outcomes: reliable monetary transfer under fixed supply rules, and convergence on one ledger through the combination of computational work and independent validation.

Non-Consensus Alternatives and Their Limits

The rejection of BIP-110 returned the network to the set of tools available outside consensus rule changes. These tools address aspects of data volume, node costs, and legal exposure, yet each operates under significant constraints that explain both their continued use and the recurring pressure for stronger measures.

Mempool and relay policy remains the most widely deployed instrument. Node software can refuse to propagate or accept into its mempool transactions that exceed size, data-carrier, or script limits defined as standard. Bitcoin Core’s historical OP_RETURN policy and the stricter defaults maintained by clients such as Bitcoin Knots exemplify this approach. Policy reduces the visibility and relay success of disfavored constructions across large portions of the network. Its limit is structural: a miner who receives a high-fee transaction directly, or who chooses to bypass policy, can still include it in a valid block. Once that block accumulates sufficient work, nodes following the heaviest chain must accept the data.

Worst-Case Scenarios Post-BIP-110

  • 03
    Expanded and compounding legal surface area for archival nodes: The most convenient vectors for embedding material that creates possession or distribution liability remain open.   • 04

  • 06

Recommendation: Principles for a Successor Proposal

Directly countering the failure modes and coordination friction observed during the August 2026 event, any future attempt to address permanent data externalities at the consensus layer must be governed by strict design countermeasures. The analysis yields a concrete set of criteria intended to raise the procedural and empirical quality of subsequent proposals rather than to force premature adoption.

  • High Legitimacy Thresholds: A signaling requirement in the range of 90 to 95 percent of blocks within a difficulty period ensures that activation reflects broad hashrate commitment.
  • Measurable Externalities: Any proposal must rest on clear, publicly auditable evidence that the targeted externality has reached a material scale.
  • Narrow Scope & Temporary Nature: Restrictions should target specific constructions responsible for the largest externalities and incorporate a mandatory sunset after a defined block height.
  • Grandfathering: Existing ownership rights must be protected; any UTXO created before activation must remain spendable under prior rules.
  • No Forced Partitions: Activation mechanics must avoid designs that compel enforcing nodes to reject the heaviest chain while support remains negligible.
  • Cost-Based Limits: Preference should be given to mechanisms that raise the cost of high-externality constructions rather than rendering broad classes categorically invalid.

Sketch of a New BIP Framework

Drawing on the principles established in the preceding section, a successor framework would treat consensus-level responses to permanent data externalities as an exceptional measure subject to elevated procedural and empirical requirements. The following sketch outlines the core elements of such a framework, referred to here as Resource Externalities Soft Fork Guidelines.

The purpose of any proposal advanced under the guidelines would be narrowly defined: to reduce measurable, sustained externalities imposed on the archival node network by specific high-data transaction constructions, for a limited period, without disturbing ordinary monetary transfers or existing ownership rights.

Activation, Measurement, and Sunset Requirements

The framework outlined above requires precise operational rules for measurement, activation, and termination if it is to avoid the legitimacy and process failures of BIP-110.

Pre-activation measurement would last a minimum of three full difficulty periods, approximately six weeks. During this window a standardized set of metrics would be published at the end of each period: the percentage of block weight occupied by the constructions targeted, absolute volume in bytes, growth rates, and estimated incremental storage burden.

Once active, the same metric suite used in the pre-activation phase would continue to be published at the end of each difficulty period. These reports would serve both as ongoing evaluation of the intervention’s effect and as the empirical basis for any later decision about extension. The additional consensus rules would remain in force only until a predetermined block height, set at the time of activation and corresponding to a period of approximately one year or less. At that height the rules would expire automatically and the prior validity conditions would resume without further action.

Conclusion and Open Questions

The BIP-110 conflict of 2026 supplies a clear case study in the limits of soft-fork governance when incentives diverge and legitimacy is thin. The proposal sought temporary consensus restrictions on high-data constructions in order to reduce permanent storage costs and legal exposure borne by archival nodes. Its activation design combined a lowered signaling threshold with a mandatory rejection window. When that window opened with approximately 2.5 percent prior support, enforcing nodes formed a minority chain that advanced only two blocks. The dominant chain, secured by the overwhelming majority of hashrate and recognized by economic nodes, continued under existing rules.

Two conclusions follow. First, activation under the observed levels of support would have damaged Bitcoin’s consensus legitimacy more than the rejection that occurred. Soft forks that alter validity conditions for the entire network require broad and durable agreement; BIP-110 never approached that condition. Second, the rejection left the underlying asymmetry intact. Miners continue to capture one-time fees for inclusion while nodes permanently absorb the associated resource and legal costs. Non-consensus tools remain available, yet their limitations explain why pressure for a stronger response is likely to return.

The preceding section on logical extensions of the post-BIP-110 equilibrium makes the second conclusion concrete. Once hashrate retains an effective veto over consensus limits on permanent data externalities, a set of downstream trajectories becomes more plausible: faster archival chain growth from unrestricted high-volume data, reinforced socialization of costs onto the validating set, expanded legal surface area for node operators, deeper path dependence against later correction, and a gradual shift in the practical character of independent validation. These are not certainties. They are the cumulative implications of the incentive equilibrium that the episode confirmed. Treating them as negligible requires assuming that fee markets and local policy will adequately internalize permanent externalities that they have so far failed to internalize.

The framework sketched earlier in this paper attempts to keep a path open for future response without repeating the legitimacy failures of BIP-110. High signaling thresholds, mandatory pre-activation measurement, narrow scope, automatic sunset, and the absence of forced low-support partitions remain the minimum conditions under which any consensus-level intervention could claim compatibility with Bitcoin’s coordination mechanisms. Whether those conditions can ever be met on this issue is now an open empirical and political question.

Several further questions remain. The actual trajectory of archival node counts, geographic distribution, and resource requirements under continued unrestricted data embedding will supply the most direct evidence. The composition of fees between monetary and data demand will reveal whether the crowding-out effect intensifies. The design choices of any subsequent proposals will show whether the lessons of the mandatory-window failure have been absorbed or ignored. And the long-term relationship between Bitcoin’s monetary properties and the breadth of independent validation will determine whether the base layer continues to rest on a wide set of fully verifying participants or drifts toward greater reliance on professionalized and selective infrastructure.

Bitcoin converges on a single history through the accumulation of work and the economic recognition of one ledger under stable rules. The BIP-110 episode reaffirmed the cost of attempting to change those rules without broad support. It also confirmed that the structural tension between short-term fee extraction and long-term node viability was not resolved. The network remains free to treat the resulting trajectory as acceptable. It is no longer free to claim that the trajectory was inevitable or without consequence.

Appendix: Formal Mathematical Models

The following appendices provide a rigorous mathematical and formal treatment of the core dynamics governing the BIP-110 dispute. By translating qualitative economic externalities, asymptotic chain dynamics, and sociological consensus thresholds into precise mathematical formulations, these sections formalize the structural frictions between fee-maximizing miners and permanently burdened archival nodes. Specifically, the following models formalize the unpriced marginal cost externality and temporal asymmetry of storage burdens, apply probability theory and the heaviest-chain rule to evaluate the asymptotic survival probability and block-time divergence of a low-support minority enforcement chain, and construct a formal legitimacy function relating miner signaling thresholds to network coordination stability and the risk of adversarial partitions.

Economic Externality Formalization

Let \(R_m\) represent the miner's one-time revenue from transaction inclusion fee \(f_i\) for embedding arbitrary data of size \(s_i\):

\[R_m = \sum_{i \in \text{data}} f_i(s_i)\]

Conversely, let \(C_n\) denote the permanent ongoing cost of storage, bandwidth, and legal exposure borne perpetually by full validating archival nodes:

\[C_n = \int_{t_0}^{\infty} \left( \alpha \cdot S(t) + \beta \cdot L(t) \right) e^{-rt} dt\]

where \(S(t)\) is the cumulative state growth and \(L(t)\) is the time-varying legal risk factor. Because \(\frac{\partial R_m}{\partial C_n} = 0\) for miners post-inclusion, an unpriced externality \(\Phi\) exists:

\[\Phi = C_n - \sum_{i} \mathbb{E}[\text{Internalized Node Benefit}]\]

Minority Chain Asymptotic Game Theory

Let \(H_{dom}\) and \(H_{min}\) denote the hashrate allocated to the dominant chain and the minority enforcing chain respectively, such that \(H_{min} = \epsilon H_{dom}\) where \(\epsilon \approx 0.025\). The difficulty adjustment target \(D\) remains identical immediately following the fork:

\[\mathbb{E}[T_{block}^{min}] = \frac{D \cdot 2^{32}}{H_{min}} = \frac{1}{\epsilon} \mathbb{E}[T_{block}^{dom}] \approx 40 \text{ hours per block}\]

Under the heaviest-chain selection rule, the probability \(P_{reorg}\) of the minority chain overtaking or sustaining viability without economic coordination asymptotically approaches zero:

\[\lim_{k \to \infty} P(\text{Chain}_{min} \succ \text{Chain}_{dom}) = 0 \quad \text{for} \quad \epsilon < 0.5\]

Consensus Legitimacy Threshold Function

Let \(L(\theta)\) be the legitimacy function of a soft fork parameterized by the miner signaling threshold \(\theta \in (0, 1]\):

\[L(\theta) = 1 - \exp\left( -\frac{\theta}{\theta_0} \right) - \gamma \cdot \mathbb{I}(\text{Mandatory Window Forced})\]

To preserve network consensus stability and prevent adversarial splits, the threshold must satisfy:

\[\theta^* \geq 0.90\]
